## Dementia is an Umbrella Term

When most people hear the word "dementia", they think of Alzheimer's disease. Alzheimer's is the most common form — accounting for roughly 60–70% of cases globally — but dementia is an umbrella term that covers a spectrum of conditions, each with somewhat different causes, symptoms, and progression patterns.

Understanding the distinctions matters for caregivers and families because:
- Different types of dementia affect different functions of the brain, leading to different care needs
- Some symptoms are specific to certain types and may be alarming or confusing without context
- The timeline of progression varies significantly across types
- Occasionally, an initial diagnosis is revised as the disease progresses and more information becomes available

## Alzheimer's Disease (60–70% of cases)

**What it is:** The most common cause of dementia. Caused by amyloid plaques and tau tangles that gradually damage brain cells, beginning in the hippocampus (memory centre).

**Signature features:**
- Memory loss for recent events is usually the first and most prominent symptom
- Gradual, progressive decline over many years (average 8–12 years from diagnosis to end of life, but varies widely)
- Difficulty finding words, recognising familiar people, and managing complex tasks
- Personality and behavioural changes in middle and late stages

**What distinguishes it:** The insidious, gradual onset and the centrality of memory impairment from early in the disease.

## Vascular Dementia (15–20% of cases)

**What it is:** Caused by reduced blood flow to the brain — through strokes, mini-strokes (TIAs), or disease of the small blood vessels in the brain. Common in people with high blood pressure, diabetes, or a history of stroke.

**Signature features:**
- Symptoms sometimes appear suddenly after a stroke, rather than gradually
- Decline may be "stepwise" — periods of stability followed by sudden worsening after further vascular events
- Executive function (planning, reasoning, decision-making) is often more affected early on than memory
- Physical symptoms may accompany cognitive changes (weakness, balance problems)

**What distinguishes it:** The connection to cardiovascular health, the stepwise rather than gradual progression, and the prominence of executive dysfunction.

**India relevance:** Vascular dementia may be proportionally more common in South Asia due to high rates of hypertension, diabetes, and stroke. This is an area where lifestyle risk factor management can make a genuine difference.

## Lewy Body Dementia (approximately 10–15% of cases)

Lewy body dementia (LBD) is an umbrella term that covers two closely related diagnoses: **Dementia with Lewy Bodies (DLB)** and **Parkinson's Disease Dementia (PDD)**. Both involve abnormal protein deposits (Lewy bodies) in brain cells.

**Signature features:**
- **Visual hallucinations** — often vivid, detailed, and well-formed (seeing animals or people that are not there). These can appear relatively early and be distressing for families who don't understand their cause.
- **Fluctuating cognition** — alertness and attention vary significantly, sometimes within the same day
- **Parkinsonism** — tremor, stiffness, slowness of movement (similar to Parkinson's disease)
- **REM sleep behaviour disorder** — physically acting out dreams (sometimes for years before cognitive symptoms appear)
- **Sensitivity to antipsychotic medications** — certain medications that are sometimes used to manage behavioural symptoms in other dementias can cause severe, life-threatening reactions in people with Lewy body dementia

**What distinguishes it:** The visual hallucinations and fluctuating cognition are distinctive. The medication sensitivity is a critical safety issue.

## Frontotemporal Dementia (FTD — approximately 5–10% of cases)

FTD covers a group of conditions caused by progressive damage to the frontal and temporal lobes of the brain. It is the most common dementia in people under 65 — sometimes affecting people in their 40s and 50s.

**Signature features:**
- **Behavioural variant FTD:** Changes in personality and behaviour are prominent early on — disinhibition (saying inappropriate things), apathy, impulsive behaviour, loss of empathy, repetitive behaviours. Memory may be relatively preserved in the early stages.
- **Primary Progressive Aphasia:** Language difficulties — difficulty finding words, understanding speech, or reading and writing — are the primary early symptom.
- Does not typically begin with memory loss

**What distinguishes it:** The early prominence of behavioural or language changes rather than memory loss, and the younger age of onset.

**Why it is frequently misdiagnosed:** Personality and behavioural changes in an otherwise relatively young person may initially be attributed to psychiatric conditions (depression, bipolar disorder) before dementia is considered.

## Other Less Common Types

**Mixed dementia:** Many older people have more than one type of dementia simultaneously — most commonly Alzheimer's combined with vascular dementia. This is more common than previously recognised.

**Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus (NPH):** Caused by an abnormal accumulation of cerebrospinal fluid. Characterised by a triad of symptoms: cognitive decline, gait disturbances (a shuffling walk), and urinary incontinence. Importantly, NPH is potentially reversible with treatment.

**Creutzfeldt-Jakob Disease (CJD):** A rare, rapidly progressive prion disease. Mentioned here only because its speed of decline (weeks to months, rather than years) can be alarming and confusing if the family does not have context.

## Why the Specific Diagnosis Matters

While all dementias share the feature of progressive cognitive decline, the type of dementia affects:

1. **Medication decisions** — particularly the critical contraindications for Lewy body dementia
2. **What symptoms to expect** — hallucinations are common in Lewy body dementia but unusual in Alzheimer's; behavioural changes that appear alarming may be a core feature of FTD rather than a crisis
3. **Caregiver preparation** — different types require different adaptation over time
4. **Research trial eligibility** — clinical trials are often specific to certain dementia diagnoses

If a family member has received a dementia diagnosis, it is worth asking the specialist: "What specific type of dementia is this, and what does that mean for what we can expect?"
