## Understanding Alzheimer's Disease

Alzheimer's disease is the most common form of dementia — a term that describes a group of symptoms affecting memory, thinking, and social abilities severely enough to interfere with daily life. It is not a normal part of ageing, though it becomes more common as people grow older.

In India, an estimated 5.3 million people live with dementia, and the number is expected to grow significantly over the next two decades as the population ages. Yet awareness remains low, and many families navigate the journey of caregiving without adequate information or support.

This article is a starting point: a grounded, honest introduction to what Alzheimer's disease is, how it progresses, and what it means for the person diagnosed and their family.

## Alzheimer's vs. Normal Ageing: What's the Difference?

It is normal to occasionally forget where you put your keys or blank on a name for a moment. This type of forgetfulness tends to improve with prompting — someone mentions the name, and you remember.

Alzheimer's disease is different in important ways:

- **Forgetting recent events entirely** — not just misplacing keys, but forgetting that a conversation happened an hour ago
- **Getting lost in familiar places** — not navigating an unfamiliar city, but losing the way to a shop visited weekly for years
- **Difficulty with familiar tasks** — struggling to prepare a familiar recipe, manage money, or follow the thread of a television programme
- **Confusion about time** — not knowing the day of the week is common in older adults; not knowing what year it is, or believing long-dead relatives are still alive, is not
- **Language difficulties** — finding words less often, in ways that noticeably affect conversation
- **Changes in personality or behaviour** — uncharacteristic suspicion, withdrawal, or mood swings that persist

The key question is always: is this affecting daily functioning in a way it didn't before?

## What Happens in the Brain

Alzheimer's disease involves the gradual breakdown of brain cells (neurons) and the connections between them. Two abnormal protein structures are central to the disease process:

**Amyloid plaques** — clumps of a protein fragment called beta-amyloid that build up between nerve cells.

**Tau tangles** — twisted strands of another protein, tau, that form inside dying nerve cells.

These changes typically begin in the hippocampus — the region of the brain responsible for forming new memories — which is why memory for recent events is usually the first thing affected. Over time, the damage spreads to other parts of the brain, affecting language, reasoning, and eventually basic bodily functions.

Importantly, these brain changes begin many years — sometimes decades — before symptoms are noticeable. This is why ongoing research into early detection is significant.

## The Stages of Alzheimer's Disease

Alzheimer's generally progresses through three broad stages, though the pace and pattern vary considerably from person to person.

### Early Stage (Mild)

The person can still live independently and participate in most activities. Common signs include:
- Forgetting recent conversations or events
- Difficulty finding the right word
- Occasionally losing familiar objects
- Some difficulty with complex planning or organisation
- Mild mood or personality changes

At this stage, many people with Alzheimer's are aware that something has changed. This self-awareness can be painful, and emotional support from family is particularly important.

### Middle Stage (Moderate)

This is typically the longest stage and often the most demanding for caregivers. The person requires increasing support with daily activities. Common signs include:
- Significant memory gaps — forgetting names of close family members, major life events
- Increasing confusion about time, place, and identity
- Difficulty with personal care (dressing, bathing, toileting)
- Behavioural changes: wandering, agitation, repetitive questioning, sundowning (increased confusion in the evening)
- Sleep disturbances

### Late Stage (Severe)

The person requires full-time care and assistance with all daily activities. Physical abilities decline significantly. The person may lose the ability to speak in sentences, recognise close family, or swallow safely. At this stage, comfort, dignity, and managing pain and distress become the central goals of care.

## What Causes Alzheimer's Disease?

The exact cause is not fully understood, but research has identified several contributing factors:

**Age** is the greatest risk factor. Alzheimer's disease is rare before 65; risk doubles roughly every five years after that.

**Genetics** plays a role. Having a parent or sibling with Alzheimer's increases risk. A specific gene, APOE-e4, is associated with higher risk, though carrying it does not guarantee the disease.

**Lifestyle and health factors** — cardiovascular health, physical activity, social engagement, cognitive stimulation, and control of conditions like high blood pressure and diabetes — all influence risk. This is a reason for cautious optimism: some risk factors can be modified.

**Head injury** — a history of significant traumatic brain injury appears to increase risk.

## Getting a Diagnosis in India

If you are concerned about memory changes in yourself or a family member, the right first step is a consultation with a doctor. Depending on where you are in India, your options may include:

- **A general physician or family doctor** — can do an initial assessment and refer you to a specialist
- **A neurologist** — specialises in brain and nervous system disorders
- **A geriatrician** — specialises in the health of older adults
- **A geriatric psychiatrist** — specialises in mental health conditions in older adults, including dementia

Major cities have memory clinics and dementia assessment centres, including NIMHANS in Bangalore and AIIMS in Delhi. ARDSI (Alzheimer's and Related Disorders Society of India) maintains a network of support groups and can help connect families with resources in their region.

A diagnosis typically involves a medical history, cognitive testing (standardised tests of memory and thinking), blood tests to rule out other causes, and sometimes brain imaging.

## What Alzheimer's Is Not

Given how little open conversation there is about dementia in India, it is worth clarifying some common misunderstandings:

- **It is not "just old age"** — while age is a risk factor, Alzheimer's is a disease, not an inevitable consequence of growing older
- **It is not contagious** — you cannot catch dementia from another person
- **It is not caused by weakness of character or a spiritual failing** — stigma around dementia in India causes real harm to patients and families; the disease is physiological
- **It is not the only dementia** — vascular dementia, Lewy body dementia, and frontotemporal dementia are distinct conditions with different presentations
- **A diagnosis is not a reason to immediately withdraw from life** — in the early stages, people with Alzheimer's can and do continue to participate in family life, maintain relationships, and find meaning

## For Caregivers: What This Means for Your Family

An Alzheimer's diagnosis affects the whole family, not just the person diagnosed. Caregiving is a long journey that requires practical preparation, emotional resilience, and help.

Some starting points:

- **Learn about the disease** — understanding what to expect at each stage reduces the shock of change as it happens
- **Plan ahead** — while the person diagnosed can still participate, discuss legal matters (power of attorney), financial plans, and care preferences
- **Connect with others** — ARDSI chapters in many cities run support groups for caregivers; speaking with others on the same journey is invaluable
- **Look after yourself** — caregiver burnout is real and serious; your own health matters
