## The First Days After a Diagnosis

Receiving a dementia diagnosis — for yourself or a family member — is a defining moment. The days immediately following can feel overwhelming: a mix of grief, confusion, fear about the future, and often, a strange sense of relief that the uncertainty has finally resolved into something concrete.

This guide is for the weeks and months after the diagnosis. It is practical rather than theoretical, shaped by the realities of navigating healthcare and caregiving in India.

There is no single correct path, and every family situation is different. What follows is a framework — not a prescription.

## Give Yourself Time to Process

Before making any decisions, allow space for the emotional reality. A dementia diagnosis is a life-changing event. The person diagnosed, and every member of their family, will need time to absorb what this means.

Some common emotional responses in the early weeks:

- **Grief** — mourning the future that was imagined, and the relationship as it was
- **Denial** — difficulty accepting the diagnosis, especially if the symptoms still seem mild
- **Relief** — if the diagnosis follows years of concerning symptoms, finally having a name for it brings clarity
- **Guilt** — many family members feel guilty for past impatience or for the relief they feel
- **Fear** — about what the future holds, about capability to care, about finances, about their own risk

All of these are normal. None of them require immediate action. If distress is severe, speaking with a mental health professional — or calling a support line — is a reasonable step. See the helplines listed on this site.

## Practical Steps: The First Month

### 1. Understand the Diagnosis Clearly

Make a follow-up appointment with the diagnosing specialist and come prepared with questions. Consider bringing another family member to help remember and record what is said.

Questions to ask:
- What specific type of dementia has been diagnosed?
- What stage is it currently at?
- What symptoms should we expect in the next six to twelve months?
- Are there any medications being recommended, and what are the expected benefits and side effects?
- Are there any clinical trials we should know about?
- What follow-up appointments are needed?
- Where can we find caregiver support in our city?

### 2. Share the Information Within the Family

In Indian family contexts, this is rarely straightforward. Decisions about what to tell elderly parents, whether to disclose to extended family, how to handle the social dimension — these are real challenges without universal answers.

Some principles that may help:
- The person diagnosed has the right to be part of decisions about disclosure, while they retain that capacity
- Hiding a diagnosis from close family members often creates additional stress and limits the support available
- Children and teenagers in the household generally do better with age-appropriate honesty than with vague reassurances that something is wrong

### 3. Legal and Financial Planning — Act Early

This is the most time-sensitive practical step, and the one that many families delay until it becomes urgent. When the person diagnosed still has full legal capacity to make decisions, certain legal and financial arrangements should be made.

**Power of Attorney:** In India, a General or Specific Power of Attorney can authorise a family member or trusted person to manage financial and legal affairs on the person's behalf. While the person has capacity, they can grant this voluntarily. Once capacity is significantly impaired, the process becomes more complex and may require court intervention.

**Will and succession:** If a will has not been made, this is an important time to do so, with the help of a lawyer.

**Bank and investment access:** Understand which accounts, fixed deposits, and investments exist. Ensure a family member has authorised access where needed.

**Insurance:** Review any existing health insurance policies and understand what is and isn't covered for dementia-related care.

Consult a qualified lawyer who has experience with elder law or elder care. This is not a cost that can be safely deferred.

### 4. Build Your Care Team

No family should try to manage dementia care entirely alone. Building a care team — even a small one — distributes the responsibility and improves the quality of care.

Potential members of a care team:
- **The diagnosing specialist** (neurologist, geriatrician, or geriatric psychiatrist) for medical management
- **A general physician** who can coordinate day-to-day health needs
- **A trained caregiver or attendant** for practical support with daily activities — particularly as the disease progresses
- **A physiotherapist** if mobility is a concern
- **A social worker** connected to a hospital or NGO who can help navigate resources

Hiring a professional caregiver in India varies significantly by city. ARDSI chapters can often provide guidance on finding trained dementia caregivers in your area.

### 5. Connect with Others Who Understand

The value of peer support — speaking with other families who are on the same journey — cannot be overstated. ARDSI runs support groups for caregivers in many cities. The Dementia India Alliance helpline (8585 990 990) can connect you with support resources.

Online communities (including closed Facebook groups for Indian dementia caregivers) can provide a space to ask questions without judgement.

## Supporting the Person Diagnosed

A dementia diagnosis belongs first to the person who has it. In the early stages, the person is typically aware, feeling the full weight of what the diagnosis means, and watching for their family's reaction.

Some of the most important things a family can do in the early period:

- **Include the person in planning conversations** — for as long as possible, the person with dementia should be part of decisions about their own life
- **Listen to what they want** — their wishes about care, about where they want to live, about what matters to them, should be recorded and respected
- **Maintain normality** — continue shared activities, outings, family meals; don't treat the diagnosis as an immediate endpoint
- **Avoid talking about the person as if they are not there** — even when cognition is impaired, people with dementia retain emotional sensitivity
- **Don't correct every mistake** — gentle redirection is usually more kind and effective than correction, especially as the disease progresses

## Looking After Yourself: Caregiver Health

Caregiving for someone with dementia is a years-long journey that takes a significant physical and emotional toll. Caregiver burnout is not a character flaw — it is a predictable consequence of sustained, demanding care without adequate support.

From the beginning, build habits and structures that protect your own health:

- **Sleep** — caregiving that disrupts your sleep is not sustainable. Address sleep disturbances in the person diagnosed early.
- **Your own medical care** — don't defer your own health needs because of caregiving demands
- **Social connection** — isolation is a significant risk for caregivers. Maintain at least some connections outside the caregiving role
- **Ask for help** — from family members, from community, from professional support. Many caregivers — particularly daughters and daughters-in-law in Indian contexts — carry a disproportionate share of the burden. This should be named and addressed within families.
- **Know the signs of caregiver burnout** — persistent exhaustion, resentment, depression, anxiety, and neglect of the person being cared for are warning signs that additional support is urgently needed

## Resources in India

- **ARDSI** (Alzheimer's and Related Disorders Society of India): ardsi.org — the national body for dementia in India, with chapters in many cities
- **Dementia India Alliance helpline:** 8585 990 990
- **Tele-MANAS** (national mental health support): 14416

- **NIMHANS** (National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences, Bangalore): a leading dementia research and clinical centre
